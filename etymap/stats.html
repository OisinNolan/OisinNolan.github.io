Linguistics Jargon Glossary
Headword
A word that has its own dictionary entry (take), as opposed to an inflected form (took) or a phrase.
Etymology
The history of a word: where it came from and how it changed.
Inherited
Passed down from parent to child language within the same family, e.g. from Old English to Middle English to English.
Borrowed (a loanword)
Taken over from a different language, e.g. English coffee from Dutch koffie.
Cognates
Words in two languages that were each inherited from the same ancestor word, like English water and Dutch water, or fish and vis. Cognates are relatives, not translations: they can drift apart in meaning.
Common ancestor
The most recent word that both words descend from.
Proto-Germanic, Proto-West Germanic, Proto-Indo-European
Ancestor languages that were never written down. Scholars reconstruct their words by comparing the languages descended from them, and mark these reconstructed words with an asterisk (*watar). Proto-West Germanic is the shared ancestor of English, Dutch, German and Frisian (roughly 200 BCE to 500 CE). Proto-Germanic is the older ancestor of all Germanic languages, including the Scandinavian ones. Proto-Indo-European is older still: the ancestor of most European languages and of many in Iran and India.
Native Germanic
A word that goes back through the Germanic family itself rather than being borrowed from Latin, French and so on.
Language stage
A period in a language's history, such as Old English (c. 450–1150) or Middle Dutch (c. 1150–1500).

Where do English and Dutch words come from?

For each headword, the first language outside its own line (English or Dutch and their older stages) that it can be traced to. Percentages are out of headwords that have an etymology on Wiktionary.

Related words that aren't translations of each other

Some words have a cognate in the other language that is not their translation. Dutch klein means "small", but its English cognate is clean. They are ranked by how common the words are and split into two groups: cognates whose meaning drifted apart, and cognates that still mean the same thing but are no longer the usual word for it.

Found automatically from Wiktionary's lists of descendant words, so a few are wrong. This usually happens where two different words are spelled the same (homographs).
