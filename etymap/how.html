How etymap works

Some graph theory, some text parsing, and a few tricks to fit it all on a static website.

When you type Dutch hond, the page shows its English relatives: hound, which shares a Proto-West Germanic ancestor, and further back canine and cynic, which meet it in Proto-Indo-European. To get there, the build turns a dictionary into a graph, traces every word's ancestors, and indexes the result so that any two words that share an ancestor can be found at once. Everything is computed ahead of time, so your browser only downloads a few small files.

1. Building the graph

English Wiktionary writes etymologies as prose, but the prose is built from structured templates. The entry for Dutch nemen reads "From Middle Dutch nēmen, from Old Dutch neman, from Proto-West Germanic *neman…", and underneath it looks like this:

{{inh|nl|dum|nēmen}}, from {{inh|nl|odt|neman}}, from {{inh|nl|gmw-pro|*neman}} …

inh means inherited (the word passed down within a language family), bor means borrowed, der means derived, and so on. The Wiktextract project parses all of Wiktionary into JSON. The build streams the full dump (about 11 million entries in a 3 GB file) and keeps entries in around 50 relevant languages.

Each word becomes a node keyed by language|normalised form (macrons are stripped except in reconstructions, to match how Wiktionary names its pages). Each template becomes a directed edge child → parent. Two details make this harder than it looks:

  • Where does a chain stop? Every template on the nemen page says "nemen comes from X", not "the previous word comes from X". So the parser links consecutive templates only when the text between them ends in a connective such as "from" or "via". Text like "Compare", "Cognate with" or "Alternatively" breaks the chain. Otherwise take's "…from Old Norse taka. Alternatively, from Proto-West Germanic *takan" would turn into a single nonsense chain.
  • Trust tiers. A word's own page is the best evidence about its parent. Next best are the descendants trees on ancestor pages: the Proto-Germanic page for *nemaną lists every known daughter form, including Old English niman, whose line no modern dictionary page would mention. The weakest evidence is a link inferred from the middle of someone else's chain. When choosing a parent, the build takes the best tier available, and within a tier it prefers inherited over borrowed over derived.

One more repair: Latin and Greek participles often have their own pages that say only "form of" their verb. English subject comes from Latin subiectus, and Dutch onderwerp is a calque (a part-by-part translation) of subiectum. Both are forms of subiciō "to throw under", so the build adds an edge from each form to its lemma, and the two words meet there.

The result is about 1.8 million nodes and 2.9 million edges.

2. Tracing a word's ancestors

Starting from a word, the build walks parent edges breadth-first. Most of the time this gives a simple chain: water ← Middle English water ← Old English wæter ← Proto-West Germanic *watar ← … But compounds have several parents (Dutch tijdperk is tijd + perk), so the ancestry is a directed acyclic graph. Affixes (-je, be-, *-aną) are drawn but never followed. If they were, almost any two verbs would "share an ancestor" in the Proto-Germanic infinitive ending. Each DAG is capped at 32 nodes, which breadth-first order makes a graceful limit: the nodes that get cut are the most distant ones.

3. Finding the common ancestor

Write A(x) for the set of ancestors of x (including x itself). A Dutch word d and an English word e are related when C = A(d) ∩ A(e) is non-empty. Ancestry is a partial order, and the lowest common ancestors are the minimal elements of C: the common ancestors that have no other common ancestor below them.

In a tree the LCA is unique, and classic algorithms answer LCA queries in constant time after linear preprocessing. In a DAG there can be several minimal common ancestors: a compound and its translation might share two roots that are unrelated to each other (nightmare meets Dutch nachtmerrie in both *naht and *marā). The general problem is much harder. Bender et al., "Lowest common ancestors in trees and directed acyclic graphs" (2005), relate the all-pairs version to fast matrix multiplication. Here each DAG has a median of 12 nodes, so the direct method works fine: compute C, then drop every element that is an ancestor of another element of C. The survivors are ranked by combined distance to the two words.

Two special cases fall out of this for free:

  • Loanwords. If the LCA is one of the two words itself, one word was borrowed from the other. English yacht descends from Dutch jacht, so the LCA of (jacht, yacht) is jacht. The same goes for vrolijk → frolic.
  • Different routes. table and tafel share the LCA Latin tabula, but one path runs through Old French and the other through an early Germanic borrowing. When any edge on either path is a borrowing, the summary says "by different routes".

A judgement call: Wiktionary sometimes has several unrelated words with the same spelling ("Etymology 1", "Etymology 2"). The build traces each one separately, and each relative remembers which of them it belongs to, so bank "bench" and bank "river bank" get their own relatives.

4. Finding every relative

Comparing a word with all 76,000 English or 51,000 Dutch words one by one would be slow. Instead the build inverts the ancestry: an index maps every ancestor to the words that descend from it. For a word d, each node of A(d) is looked up in the other language's index, and every word found there is a relative. The true LCA of each pair is then computed as above. Relatives are grouped by their LCA and ordered from the closest group (Proto-West Germanic, say) to the most distant (Proto-Indo-European). The groups are filled breadth-first, two per ancestor before any group gets more, so the distant relatives (often the most surprising) aren't crowded out by close ones. Within a group, well-known words come first.

Taken literally, "shares an ancestor" is far too generous, and most of the work is in deciding what to leave out:

  • Words built from a modern word. greyhound reaches *hundaz only through hound, so it isn't listed as a relative of hond: hound is.
  • Compounds. seawater shares *watar with Dutch water, but listing every compound would bury the real cognate. A compound only matches another compound, and then both parts must be shared (nachtmerrie ~ nightmare). A suffixed form made in another language counts as a plain step, so canine (from Latin canīnus, canis + -īnus) still reaches *ḱwṓ.
  • Proto-Indo-European suffixes. Wiktionary analyses many PIE roots as root + suffix. Following those edges would make hond a relative of oh through a shared ending, so they are never followed.
  • Rare words. Very rare words are dropped (roughly: ones Wiktionary translates into fewer than three languages), unless they were borrowed directly.

Some relatives are missed by the index because the two words' own pages never reach the same node. Dutch wiel goes back to Proto-Germanic *hwehwlą, but wheel's page stops at a different reconstruction, *hweulō. The *hwehwlą page, however, lists Old English hwēol among its descendants. So for each Germanic ancestor the build also runs a depth-first search downwards through its descendants tree. The search may only enter the other language's line, and stops at the first modern word. A relative found this way is grafted onto its own ancestry at the first node its page knows (here hwēol), so the drawn tree stays consistent.

The graph has a trap here. Nodes are keyed by spelling, so homographs merge: Middle English ey "egg" is also a spelling of other words, and an unrestricted search walks into hay, eye and any. Three guards keep this in check:

  • Each descendants tree on Wiktionary is internally consistent (one editor wrote it about one word), so the search first stays inside the tree of the ancestor it started from.
  • A branch is rejected if the relative's own page names a parent in a different language for a step on it.
  • The search only starts from Germanic ancestors: further back, Latin caseus "cheese" picked up chess and choose.

Because the browser rebuilds each pair from the two words' own ancestries (plus any graft), the tree you see is always the one that justifies the match.

No relative?

About two in five words have no relative in the other language (Dutch fiets "bicycle" is of unknown origin). For those the page suggests words close in meaning that do have one. It draws on Wiktionary's synonyms, related terms and broader terms, on topical categories (nl:Cycling), and on the translation itself. fiets translates as bicycle, which goes back to Greek kúklos, which is related to Dutch cyclus. Its synonym rijwiel leads to ride.

5. Storing the data

There is no server, so everything is precomputed. The whole build (ancestries, index, relatives and suggestions for 127,000 words) runs in about a minute, and the browser fetches only what it needs:

  • Autocomplete downloads a sorted list of headwords once (about 200 kB compressed) and binary-searches it for your prefix on every keystroke.
  • Words are stored in hash buckets: a word lives in file CRC32(word) mod 2048 of its language. The browser computes the same hash, so it knows which file to fetch without needing an index. Each entry holds the word's ancestry, its list of relatives and the display data for every node. Inflected forms are stored as redirects (liep → lopen).
  • Showing a pair costs two small requests of roughly 20–70 kB: the typed word's bucket and the relative's. The pair and its LCA are rebuilt in the browser from the two ancestries, so pairs are never stored, and any relative can be shown however distant it is.

6. The timeline, family tree and map labels

The timeline has to span from about 4500 BCE (Proto-Indo-European) to today without squashing the medieval period, where most of the action happens. It uses a logarithmic scale anchored at the present:

u(y) = 1 − ln(1 + (2026 − y)/500) / ln(1 + (2026 − ymin)/500)

Near the present this is roughly linear, and further back it compresses logarithmically. The constant 500 sets where that switch happens. Each word appears when its language stage begins, nudged so that a child never appears before its parent. Arcs grow along a quadratic Bézier curve, cut at fraction t using de Casteljau's algorithm: interpolate twice and you get the control points of the partial curve.

The family tree in the side panel uses the same time scale vertically, so scrubbing the timeline moves a "now" line down the tree. Its one adjustment is a monotone warp: if two words are closer in time than a label is tall, everything below is pushed down a little. The mapping stays strictly increasing, so it can be inverted, and you can drag on the tree to scrub.

Labels. Placing map labels so that none overlap is NP-hard in general (it is the classic point-feature label placement problem). The page uses a simple greedy approximation. Labels are sorted by importance (the two words, then the common ancestor, then the form at the current moment, then everything else). Each is tried in four positions around its dot (the "four-position model"), and skipped if they all collide. Hover any dot to see a skipped label, or any link to see how two words are connected ("English town is inherited from Middle English toun").

Languages without a place. A few hundred rare languages (and Wiktionary's "Translingual") have no map position. They still appear in the tree, and on the map the arc skips over them to the nearest ancestor that has one.

Caveats

  • Dates are the rough lifetime of a language stage, not when the word was first written down.
  • Places are approximate home regions, not migration routes. Reconstructed languages are drawn as large, faint circles, and the location of the Proto-Indo-European homeland is debated.
  • Wiktionary is excellent but uneven, and the chain-parsing heuristics sometimes misfire. Every step links back to its Wiktionary entry so you can check.
